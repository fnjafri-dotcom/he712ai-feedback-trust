Feedback and Trust in AI

This week asks what kind of feedback AI should give, what should stay human, and how we teach learners (and ourselves) to trust AI feedback as much as it deserves, no more and no less.

About 4 hours Course objectives 2, 4, 7 Predict → desirable difficulties → reflect
This week's question: If AI can give feedback on everything, what feedback should it give, and what should it leave to us?

Learning objectives

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

  1. Explain what makes feedback effective, using Hattie and Timperley's three questions and four levels, and identify where AI-generated feedback fits or falls short.
  2. Describe automation bias, disuse, and calibrated trust, and recognize them in your own responses to AI feedback.
  3. Analyze the role of AI feedback in your own course through your theory lens.
  4. Design one feedback interaction for your course project with clear roles for AI and for people, and safeguards against over-trust and under-trust.

Step 1 · Predict

Before you read anything, answer these in the ungraded D2L quiz. Committing to an answer first, even a wrong one, makes the readings stick. You'll come back to these in Step 4.

  1. On a scale of 0 to 100, how much do you trust AI-generated feedback for learners today?
  2. Which feedback level do you think AI is best at, and which is it worst at: task, process, self-regulation, or self?
  3. Would you give a learner an AI-drafted feedback note without editing it? Why or why not?

Step 2 · Read and work: desirable difficulties

For each reading, do the short activity before or while you read, then post your answer to the shared question in the D2L discussion. Answers are hidden under "Check" so you can commit to your own first.

Reading 1 · The power of feedback (35 min)

Hattie J, Timperley H. Rev Educ Res. 2007;77(1):81-112. doi:10.3102/003465430298487. Read the feedback model (the three questions and four levels) and skim the rest.

Activity: sort the feedback. Which level is each one: task, process, self-regulation, or self?

  1. "Your SBAR is missing the Recommendation. Add what you're asking the provider to do."
  2. "Before you finalize a medication list, check each drug against today's labs. That habit catches most errors."
  3. "Next time, what will you look for to know whether your handoff was complete?"
  4. "Great job, you're a natural!"
Check

(a) Task, (b) Process, (c) Self-regulation, (d) Self. AI tends to do well with task-level and process-level feedback tied to clear criteria. Self-regulation feedback depends on a back-and-forth conversation, which is where AI struggled as a debriefer (Reading 2). Self-level praise is the least useful level whoever gives it.

Shared question: Which feedback level is AI best at, and which is it worst at? Why?

Reading 2 · AI-powered simulation for telehealth communication training (20 min)

Jafri FN, Elsener M, Kumar A, Kardong-Edgren S, Edwards RA. Simul Healthc. 2026. doi:10.1097/SIH.0000000000000940. Read the debriefing findings and the discussion.

Activity: predict before you read. The team tried several debriefing approaches across AI platforms. Which do you think AI could deliver reliably: advocacy-inquiry, plus-delta, or microdebriefing with rubric-focused feedback? Write down your guess.

Check

AI couldn't reliably deliver advocacy-inquiry or plus-delta. Rubric-focused microdebriefing worked best, because it plays to AI's strength in consistent, structured feedback. A companion study found communication scores on real calls improved 19 to 37% after the training.

Shared question: Why couldn't AI do advocacy-inquiry, and what does that tell us about which feedback needs a person?

Reading 3 · Ambient AI scribes for educational feedback notes (30 min)

Talwalkar JS, Chartash D, Zhang L, et al. JMIR Med Educ. 2026;12:e89996. doi:10.2196/89996. Whole paper (11 pages).

Activity: estimate before you read. An AI scribe recorded instructors' spoken feedback to first-year medical students, and an AI model wrote it up. Guess: (1) what share of AI feedback points misrepresented what the instructor said, (2) what share were hallucinated, and (3) how much longer the AI notes were than human-only notes.

Check

(1) 6.8% mischaracterized, (2) 1.7% hallucinated, (3) about 7 times longer (a median of 344 words vs 45 to 46). Raters scored the AI-assisted notes higher on quality (median 3 vs 2 out of 5), and instructors caught most errors while editing. No one asked the students whether the notes helped them.

Shared question: The AI notes scored higher, but no one asked the students. What would you need to know before using this in your course?

Reading 4 · Ethical use of AI in health professions education (20 min)

Masters K. AMEE Guide No. 158. Med Teach. 2023. doi:10.1080/0142159X.2023.2186203. Read four sections only: algorithm bias, algorithm transparency, responsibility and accountability, and autonomy.

Activity: you decide. An AI tool tells a nursing student a dose calculation is correct. It isn't. The student repeats the error in a high-stakes simulation and fails the station. Who is most responsible: the vendor, the faculty member who assigned the tool, the institution that approved it, or the student?

See what Masters argues

There's no single right answer, and that's the point. AI offers probabilities, not answers for an individual case, so a person makes the final call, and institutions need clear policies on responsibility, accountability, blame, and credit before something goes wrong. Faculty can be blamed both for ignoring an approved tool and for blindly following it.

Shared question: If an AI feedback tool gets something wrong, who is responsible in your setting?

Through your lens

Answer the prompt for your lens group in about 200 words, drawing on at least two readings.

Cognitive loadWhen does AI feedback reduce a learner's load, and when does it add to it? (Think about a 344-word note for a first-year student.)

Step 3 · Trust Lab

Five short cases, each with AI feedback on a learner's work. Decide what you'd do with each one: use it as is, edit it, check something first, or not use it. There's no answer key. Copy your choices for the partner discussion.

Open the lab

No access to the lab? Use this text version (2 cases)

For each case, decide what you'd do with the feedback and how sure you are (1 to 5). Then open "Two ways to see it."

A. Patient education handout. A learner wrote: "Engage in therapeutic exercise 3x daily to optimize functional mobility and mitigate deconditioning." AI feedback: "This is written above most patients' reading level. Here's a plain-language version you can use: 'Do your exercises 3 times a day. They help you stay strong and move more easily.'"

Two ways to see it

For: the feedback is accurate and the rewrite is better for patients. Caution: it did the work for the learner. Will they be able to write plainly next time? Think about: when does a model answer help learning, and when does it replace it?

B. Using evidence. A learner wrote that fall prevention programs combining strength and balance exercise appear to reduce falls. AI feedback: "Good summary. To strengthen it, cite Martins et al. (2021), a Cochrane review showing a 40% reduction in falls."

Two ways to see it

For: pointing a learner to a systematic review is the right instinct. Caution: is the citation real? AI tools sometimes invent plausible references (this one was written for this exercise). Think about: what should you verify before passing AI feedback along?

Step 4 · Reflect

Submit a 300 to 400 word reflection in the D2L assignment for your Theory Experience Portfolio. Start by restating your Step 1 prediction and re-rating your trust from 0 to 100. Then address:

  1. What changed between your prediction and now, and what changed it: a reading, the lab, or a specific case?
  2. Look at your choices in the lab. Where were you most and least sure, and what does your lens explain about why?
  3. In the course you're designing, what feedback would you give to AI and what would you keep human?

Step 5 · Partner exchange

Post (about 250 words): Your lens answer and your lab summary line.

Reply to a partner from a different lens group: what did their lens notice that yours missed?

Step 6 · Apply it: the Feedback Design Card

Choose one moment in the course you're building where learners get feedback, and complete one page:

  1. The moment: where learners get feedback, and on what.
  2. The outcome: which course outcome it serves.
  3. The division of labor: what AI does and what a person does, by feedback level.
  4. Your lens: how your theory shapes the design (two or three sentences).
  5. Safeguards: one against over-trust and one against under-trust.
  6. Equity check: who this feedback might serve poorly, and what you'll do about it.
  7. Evidence: how you'll know it worked.

Instructor notes

  • Step 2: one discussion topic with a thread for each shared question, plus the lens prompt. The "Check" answers on this page use plain HTML, so they work inside a D2L content page.
